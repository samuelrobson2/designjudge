Use the findings to make one judgment about the Layout as a whole, in this order:

1. Find the most serious material weakness. Weigh it by what it affects (the primary task, or a secondary part of the interface) and where it occurs (a baseline state: desktop, tablet or mobile; or only an edge state: combined stress, one sweep width, an interactive state or a content variant).
2. If there is no material weakness, the strengths decide between 4 and 5.
3. Choose the anchor whose conditions the findings meet. Several material weaknesses that together hinder the primary task can justify the next anchor down. Otherwise, do not average criteria or count findings.

Explain which findings led to the anchor.

- 5 · Exceptional: No material weakness. Material strengths make the primary task easier than a merely sound layout would, in every baseline state: hierarchy, grouping, density and responsive changes are deliberate choices that serve the task, not only an absence of problems.
- 4 · Strong: No material weakness. The layout is clear, efficient and coherent in every required state. Minor weaknesses may remain.
- 3 · Competent: Material weaknesses are contained: each affects a secondary part of the interface, or appears only in an edge state. In every baseline state, the primary task stays readily understandable and usable.
- 2 · Poor: A material weakness affects the primary task in a baseline state, or material weaknesses recur across several states or criteria. The task remains possible but takes meaningfully more effort.
- 1 · Failing: A material weakness makes the primary task impossible to complete, or makes a required state unusable.
